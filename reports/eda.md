# What the data say (exploratory analysis)

This page summarises what we learned about the data before building the model, and how each finding shaped a modelling choice. Every number is generated by `make eda`.

## The data are complete and clean
We have 40 geos (geographic areas) observed every week for 156 weeks (2021-01-25 to 2024-01-15). Nothing is missing, nothing is duplicated, and spend, impressions and sales are never negative. The only negative numbers are in the two control indices, which are centred around zero by design.

Spend has many low weeks and a few large ones (the mean is above the median), and some channels are often off: Channel2 runs in only 36% of geo-weeks. Competitor sales and sentiment are indices centred on zero, so their negative values are expected.

Revenue per conversion barely changes (it varies by only 0.5%), so results in revenue and in conversions tell the same story. Its value (about 0.02) looks low because the simulated conversions are inflated (around 19 per person per week); revenue itself is on a realistic scale, with paid spend at 17% of revenue. Conversions and revenue per conversion should not be read literally, which is one reason results are reported in revenue.

## Meridian's standard checks
Meridian runs its own checks on the model inputs ([full report](meridian/eda.html)). Nothing blocks the model. Two checks are flagged for review, and both turn out to be harmless.

- **Enough data.** 6,240 geo-weeks for about 50 main parameters, roughly 125 data points each.
- **Spend and impressions carry the same information.** In every channel, spend is impressions times a constant CPM (Channel0 7.33, Channel1 9.64, Channel2 7.43, Channel3 7.79, Channel4 7.79). Meridian flags 200 geo-weeks as unusual CPMs, but they differ only in the sixth decimal place: rounding, not real variation. So it makes no difference whether the model uses spend or impressions.
- **Some weeks are unusually large (flagged).** Meridian flags values far from a variable's typical range: 36 geo-weeks of revenue and 1,461 media and control values (3% of them). Spend is often zero and occasionally large, so many such points are expected. They are real values, not errors, and we keep them.
- **Inputs are not too entangled.** The strongest overlap between any two inputs is a correlation of 0.60 (Channel4 and competitor_sales_control), and no input is largely explained by the others (variance inflation factor at most 2.6). The model can tell them apart.
- **Bigger geos spend more.** Each channel's spend tracks population almost perfectly (rank correlation 0.97 to 0.99), while the controls and Promo barely do. **For the model:** revenue and media are expressed per person; controls are not.
- **Inputs vary both over time and across geos.** The week alone explains at most 33% of any input's variation (Channel3), so no input is just a national time pattern that the baseline could absorb.

## Revenue grows slowly
Revenue per year (each year is a block of 52 weeks, starting in late January):

| year | weeks | revenue | growth vs previous year |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 2021-01-25 to 2022-01-17 | 433M | - |
| 2 | 2022-01-24 to 2023-01-16 | 442M | 2.1% |
| 3 | 2023-01-23 to 2024-01-15 | 444M | 0.4% |

Growth slowed: 2.1% then 0.4% a year. Part of that is noise. Weekly revenue varies by about 6.9% around its level, so a 52-week total varies by about 1.0% from noise alone, and the difference between two years' growth rates by about 1.4%. The slowdown is about that size: it may be real, but the data cannot confirm it. **For the model:** a straight-line baseline (`knots = 2`: one knot at each end of the series, no bends in between) is a reasonable simplification; letting it bend a little is one of the next steps.

## No yearly seasonality
We checked whether the same week looks similar every year (for example, a peak every summer). It does not, beyond what chance would produce: random noise of the same length shows a pattern at least this strong 28% of the time. **For the model:** no seasonal component is needed.

Looking at each geo separately, 6 of 40 show a yearly pattern, where about 2 would by chance. A few geos may have their own seasonality, but it does not add up to a shared yearly cycle nationally.

<details><summary>Technical details</summary>

- Week-of-year means explain R2 = 0.360 of detrended national revenue. Pure noise of the same length gives 0.330 on average (95th percentile 0.418); p = 0.28 (iid null), 0.28 (AR(1) null, phi = -0.025).
- Per geo, 6 of 40 geos are significant at 5% (2 expected by chance); the probability of that many or more by chance is p = 0.014.

</details>

## How noisy is revenue week to week?
A typical geo's weekly revenue moves up and down by about 30% around its average; nationally, where geos offset each other, by 6.9%. These movements look random: one week tells you almost nothing about the next. **For the model:** this sets the bar for accuracy. Even a simple 13-week average misses a typical geo-level week by 23% and a national week by 5.1%, so the model cannot be expected to do much better.

<details><summary>Technical details</summary>

- Geo-level weekly revenue: CV median 30.0% (range 21.5%-49.4%); lag-1 autocorrelation of detrended revenue median 0.007; MAPE of a 13-week moving average 23.1%.
- National weekly revenue: CV 6.9%, lag-1 autocorrelation -0.025, MAPE of a 13-week moving average 5.1%.
- Noise vs geo size: Spearman -0.33 between per-capita revenue noise and population; the quietest geo's noise is 0.60x the noisiest's. The model uses one noise level for all geos.

</details>

## Media's effect is hard to see in the raw data
Within each geo, weeks with more spend do not clearly have more revenue: every correlation between spend and revenue is tiny (at most 0.08 in size), whether we look at the same week or up to 8 weeks later. That does not mean media has no effect; it means the effect is small relative to the noise, and the model has to pool information across geos to find it. **For the model:** media effects are pooled strongly across geos, and the data do not tell us how long media effects last (we use up to 4 weeks).

- All within-geo correlations are small (|r| <= 0.079; 95% band under zero correlation +/-0.025).
- Same-week correlations with geo averages removed: Channel0 +0.032, Channel1 -0.011, Channel2 -0.050, Channel3 -0.052, Channel4 -0.079; after removing the controls, Promo and organic: Channel0 +0.001, Channel1 +0.013, Channel2 +0.014, Channel3 +0.024, Channel4 +0.011. - Lags (weeks) where the control-adjusted correlation is above the noise band: Channel0 [7]; Channel1 [7]; Channel2 [4]; Channel3 [1]; Channel4 [2, 3, 6]. No clear decay pattern, so the data do not settle the maximum lag.

</details>

## How much room there is to spend more
Most weeks run well below each geo's highest-ever spend, and some channels are often off (Channel2 is off in 64% of geo-weeks). **For budget scenarios:** raising last year's spend by 20% stays within past levels in almost all geo-weeks; by 50%, some channels go beyond anything observed, where results rely on the assumed shape of the response curve.

- Share of geo-weeks with zero spend: Channel0 12.1%, Channel1 27.9%, Channel2 64.3%, Channel3 2.7%, Channel4 13.1%.
- Median non-zero week as a share of the geo's maximum: Channel0 0.35, Channel1 0.29, Channel2 0.24, Channel3 0.41, Channel4 0.34.

## Which controls to include, and why
A control belongs in the model if it moves with both our spend and our revenue: leaving it out would credit its effect to media. The four non-media variables make that case with different strength.

- **Competitor sales: clearly needed.** Without the controls, same-week spend and revenue are negatively linked for 4 of 5 channels (down to -0.08); once the controls are accounted for, those negative links disappear (all between +0.00 and +0.02). That is the signature of a confounder: competitor sales move with our spend (correlation +0.07 to +0.55 across channels) and against our revenue (correlation -0.20), the strongest links of any input. (This before-and-after check removes all four variables together; competitor sales is the one with that pattern.)
- **Promo: kept, mainly because it runs alongside paid media.** It moves with Channel0 spend (correlation +0.33), so leaving it out would credit part of its lift to Channel0, and it is a lever the client controls. Its own link with revenue (correlation +0.13) is small, but clearly beyond chance (+/-0.025) and stronger than any media channel's (at most 0.08).
- **Sentiment: kept as a precaution.** It moves with spend (correlation +0.26 to +0.51 across channels) but barely with revenue (correlation -0.03), so the evidence that it matters is weak.
- **Organic media: kept as a precaution.** Same pattern: it moves with spend (correlation +0.28 to +0.45 across channels) but barely with revenue (correlation +0.03).

Keeping a variable that turns out not to matter costs little. But if advertising itself drives sentiment or organic activity, controlling for them removes part of media's true effect, so keeping them could understate paid media. The data cannot tell which way the influence runs. **For the model:** all four are included; whether sentiment and organic change the results is worth checking with a refit without them.
